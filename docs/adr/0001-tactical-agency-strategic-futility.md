# 0001. Player skill saves individuals; it cannot end the beef

- **Status:** Accepted
- **Date:** 2026-07-24

## Context

*Our Block* is pitched as a shooter built on high-stakes choices, in which a native
member of a **set** fights to protect it from a **rival set**. That premise forces an
early answer to one question, because every mission, encounter, and ending inherits it:
does shooting actually protect anyone?

Three options were genuinely on the table.

**Power fantasy.** Skill protects the set outright — play well, keep your people alive,
win the beef. Mechanically clean and commercially conventional, but it makes the
high-stakes pitch hollow. Nothing is at stake if competence resolves it, and the result
is a well-dressed cover shooter.

**Pure tragedy.** Retaliation always escalates and the set dies regardless of play. It is
the most honest reflection of the world the game draws from, but it carries a real design
risk: if player skill changes nothing, players correctly perceive the outcome as rigged,
resent it, and come to feel the game exists to punish them for using its own core verb.

**Split the scope of competence.** Skill is real, but narrower than the player assumes.

The decision matters now rather than later because it is not a tuning value. It dictates
mission structure, the space of possible endings, and the tuning philosophy for every
encounter in the game.

## Decision

We will make tactical skill genuinely effective at the scale of a single encounter, and
strategically inert at the scale of the campaign.

Playing well saves the specific person in front of you, immediately and legibly — a named
member of the set makes it home tonight because of something the player did. That agency
is real and must always read as real.

At the campaign scale, every exchange feeds **the cycle**. Each retaliation the player
wins produces the cause of the next one, and the aggregate cost to the set rises no
matter how well the player performs.

The player is therefore never deceived about whether their skill works. They are wrong
only about what it accomplishes. They believe they are defending; they are escalating.
Because they keep winning, they never receive the feedback that would correct them.

## Consequences

**Easier.** Encounters can be tuned for maximum tactical satisfaction with no thematic
guilt, because moment-to-moment mastery is supposed to feel good and supposed to work.
The tragedy is carried by the strategy layer, so players cannot dismiss it as the game
cheating them in a firefight. Choices gain real weight, since the player's wins are what
drive the cost upward.

**Harder.** The ending must pay this off without reading as a lecture, which is the
hardest writing in the project. The game now has a point of view, which will alienate
part of the audience arriving for a straightforward power fantasy. Encounter design must
consistently attach named, known characters to outcomes, or the individual-scale agency
stops landing and only the futility survives.

**Foreclosed.** Crime-empire progression — expanding territory, growing the set,
consolidating power — is off the table. Any structure in which the player can win the
beef outright contradicts this decision.

**Worth revisiting if.** Playtesting shows players read strategic escalation as the game
ignoring their input rather than as tragedy. That would mean the individual-scale agency
is not landing hard enough, and the fix is to strengthen it — not to abandon the cycle.
